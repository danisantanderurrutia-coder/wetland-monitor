1.1 Wetlands
Wetlands are ecosystems characterized by the presence of water on the surface or in the subsoil for extended periods, generating anoxic (no oxygen) conditions that determine physical, chemical, and biological processes. In other words “ecosystems in which water is a predominant factor that controls the environment, biota, and soil properties” (Mitsch & Gosselink, 2015).
Wetlands are distinguished by a complex interaction between hydrology, vegetation, hydromorphic soils, and microbial activity, elements that condition key processes such as primary productivity, organic matter decomposition, and the biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur. 
From a biogeochemical perspective, saturated wetland soils favor reducing conditions, limiting the aerobic mineralization of organic matter (ref.) and allowing the accumulation of carbon in the form of peat or organic sediments, particularly in temperate and boreal regions (Bridgham et al., 2013).
The spatial and temporal heterogeneity of wetlands, which comes from fluctuations in water level, redox gradients, and vegetation variability generates multiple ecological niches that foster high functional and taxonomic diversity (ref.). That's why, despite covering approximately just 6–7% of the Earth's land surface, wetlands house a high proportion of global biodiversity (Davidson et al., 2018). 
They function as critical habitats for aquatic and semi-aquatic species, plants, invertebrates, fishes, amphibians, migratory birds, and mammals (ref.). In temperate regions of Europe, coastal wetlands and peatlands are particularly important as breeding, feeding, and resting areas for migratory birds (ref.), many of which are protected by European conservation directives (ref.).[j]
According to the Ramsar Convention, the Intergovernmental international treaty dedicated to the conservation and wise use of wetlands:
“Wetlands are areas of marsh, fen, peatland or water, whether natural or artificial, permanent or temporary, with water that is static or flowing, fresh, brackish or salt, including areas of marine water the depth of which at low tide does not exceed six meters” (Ramsar Convention Secretariat, 2018).
	

This definition implies that rivers, lagoons, lakes, estuaries, deltas, inland water bodies, and shallow coastal areas are considered wetlands. 
1.1.1 Peatlands
Peatlands are a specific type of wetland defined by their ability to accumulate dead plant matter (peat) due to waterlogged conditions that prevent full decomposition. While all peatlands are wetlands, not all wetlands are peatlands. 
While wetlands is broad term for ecosystems where water covers the soil or is near the surface (e.g., swamps, marshes, bogs, fens, mangroves), peatlands are subset of wetlands characterized by waterlogged, acidic conditions that create a thick layer of organic soil (peat), typically at least 30-40 cm deep (ref.). Peatlands are generally divided into bogs (fed by rain, low nutrients[k]) and fens (fed by groundwater, higher nutrients[l]).

	Bogs are "ombrotrophic," meaning they receive water and nutrients almost exclusively from precipitation (rain and snow). Highly acidic, typically with a pH between 3.5 and 4.5; extremely nutrient-poor because they lack mineral-rich groundwater; dominated by Sphagnum moss (peat moss), which actively acidifies its environment. Often formed in "raised" domes that sit above the surrounding landscape, cutting them off from local groundwater (ref.).
Fens are "minerotrophic," receiving water that has been in contact with mineral-rich soils or bedrock via groundwater and surface runoff. Less acidic than bogs; they can be neutral or even alkaline depending on the local geology (like limestone); generally richer in nutrients than bogs, though "poor fens" exist as a middle ground (ref.). They support a much wider variety of life, including sedges, grasses, reeds, wildflowers, and even shrubs or small trees. Usually found in low-lying areas, depressions, or on slopes where groundwater naturally seeps out (ref).
Peat is partially decayed plant material that accumulates over thousands of years on peatlands. Healthy peatlands provide habitats for specialized species, including carnivorous plants, and filter water, reducing flood risks (ref.).

	1.1.2 Vegetation
Macrophytes and vascular vegetation in wetlands are essential structural and functional components that influence the hydrological, biogeochemical, and ecological processes of these ecosystems. These plants modulate organic matter accumulation, oxygen distribution in the soil and GHG fluxes between the soil and the atmosphere (Tak et al., 2023; Vroom et al., 2022; Ge et al., 2024) participating of processes as methanogenesis and methanotrophy (Li et al., 2024).

	Vegetation acts as a bridge between atmospheric carbon fixation by photosynthesis and the conservation of that carbon in the soil profile, even after death. The decaying organic matter is a fundamental substrate for microbial processes that control both aerobic and anaerobic carbon mineralization, affecting CH₄ production and consumption (Tak et al., 2023).

	Many wetland plants develop aerenchyma, a type of specialized tissue, responsible for transporting oxygen from the aerial parts to the roots, under the anoxic conditions characteristic of saturated soils, where microbial methane oxidation can occur before the gas is released into the atmosphere (Vroom et al., 2022; Armstrong, 1980). This gas conduit can be seen as a chimney for methane transport, facilitating the diffusion of CH₄ produced in deep layers to the surface without being oxidized, which can increase net emissions of this gas (Tak et al., 2023; Vroom et al., 2024). Environmental conditions determine the formation of aerenchyma (Yamauchi & Nakazono, 2022) and its air spaces can be formed as a stress response to flooding, drought or nutrient deficiency (Yamauchi & Nakazono, 2022).

	A symbiotic association between fungi, roots, and rhizomes forms a living matrix in wetland soils, providing them with structural support and a medium for communication (Shah et al., 2025; Smith, 2015; Siddiqui Z., 2008) useful for nutrient uptake, and carbon inputs through root exudates (Meier, 2019; Freschet et al., 2021). A fungus network called mycorrhizae develops through the soil linking plant roots and rhizomes, enhancing nutrient and water transport (Lindahl, 2010; Walker J. et al., 2014). Influences soil aeration patterns, nutrient availability and carbon allocation by modifying rhizosphere chemistry and affecting microbial processess, modulating methane and CO₂ fluxes (Dotaniya et al., 2015; Smith, 2015; Siddiqui Z., 2008). 
1.1.3 Water cycle
The hydrological regime of wetlands is characterized by the temporal and spatial dynamics of water inputs, storage, and output; more precisely by saturation of their soils, leading to reduced oxygen diffusion and the establishment of anaerobic conditions (ref.).
Precipitation is the main external driver of the water cycle in many wetlands, generating pulses of water inflow that can rapidly alter the water table and soil moisture content. These events can trigger transient responses in gas fluxes, such as increases in CH₄ emissions (ref.), following flooding episodes or variations in soil respiration due to changes in oxygen availability.
The presence, movement, and storage of water within the soil regulate oxygen availability, pore connectivity, and redox conditions, thus determining which microbial processes predominate and which gases are emitted or consumed, particularly regarding oxidation and methane production (ref.).
For example, high water tables limit oxygen availability, favoring anaerobic microbial pathways such as methanogenesis (ref.), whereas fluctuating water levels can create oxic–anoxic interfaces that enhance methane oxidation and influence nitrous oxide production (ref.). Therefore, water level variability is a key driver of temporal variability in GHG fluxes observed in wetland ecosystems. 	1.1.4 Soil aspects
The physical structure of soils is determined by the interaction between mineral particles, organic matter and water. High organic matter content results in soils with high porosity and low bulk density, while water saturation favors soft and highly compressible structures. This combination makes wetland soils extremely sensitive to hydrological changes such as drainage, drought, or rewetting.
Wetland soils can be considered as a biogeochemical continuum regulated by water saturation, redox conditions and microbial functional groups (ref.). They typically exhibit a vertical profile characterized by gradients in oxygen availability, redox potential, organic matter content, and microbial activity (ref.). 
The difference from wetland soils and classical terrestrial soils is that in this last ones, their vertical stratification is characterized by well-defined pedogenic horizons (ref.), resulting from long-term processes such as organic matter accumulation, eluviation, illuviation, and mineral weathering under (ref.). 
Carbon loss in classical terrestrial soils is relatively rapid, driven by aerobic microbial respiration that efficiently mineralizes organic matter to CO₂ (ref.), wetland soils, on the other hand, exhibit a fundamentally different form of stratification that is governed by hydrological control and redox dynamics (ref.). In these, organic carbon tends to be more present where plant litter inputs and microbial activity are highest, generally in upper horizons. 
  

	Figure, Symbiotic relationship between Arbuscular Mycorrhizal, Fungi and a plant root | Extracted from Maiti & Ghosh (2020).
	Water-filled pores limit gas exchange with the atmosphere and significantly reduce the rate of oxygen diffusion, favoring anaerobic processes such as fermentation, denitrification, iron and sulfate reduction, and methanogenesis (ref.). Consequently, wetland soil profiles typically exhibit relatively oxygenated surface layers and persistently reduced depths (ref.).
1.1.5 Climate change & greenhouse gases
Climate change represents the human-induced alteration of the Earth's climate system, driven by increased atmospheric concentrations of key greenhouse gases, including CO₂, CH₄, N₂O water vapor, and fluorinated gases; which trap outgoing longwave radiation and disrupt global climatic patterns (IPCC, 2021). 
This intensification of the greenhouse effect leads to severe environmental consequences, such as rising average global temperatures, heightened frequency of extreme weather events, sea level rise, ocean acidification, and widespread biodiversity loss in sensitive ecosystems like wetlands (IPCC, 2022). 
  

	Figure, Climate change and shifts in biochemical state | Altered biochemical balance and reactivated microbial metabolism accelerates the enzymatic decomposition of organic matter and the release of GHG. Extracted from Rajakaruna et al. (2024). 
	Although irreversible on human timescales, the rate of climate change can be limited through rapid reductions in greenhouse gas emissions and enhanced adaptation strategies. These efforts are guided by international policy frameworks such as the Paris Agreement, which aims to hold the increase in global average temperature well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels while pursuing efforts to limit the increase to 1.5°C (UNFCCC, 2015).
Gases such as CH₄, CO₂, and N₂O are produced in the waterlogged soils and anoxic sediments characteristic of wetlands, where anaerobic microbial processes associated with the decomposition of organic matter predominate (ref.).
Methane has a substantially higher Global Warming Potential (GWP) than CO₂, 28–34 times greater than CO₂ over a 100-year time horizon, making it a critical component in climate change projections, despite its lower atmospheric concentration and relatively short half-life (Myhre et al., 2014). 
Its high radiative efficiency and the interactions that it creates with aerosols and tropospheric ozone, produce that small variations in its emissions can generate disproportionate climate effects compared to CO₂ (Myhre et al., 2014; IPCC, 2021).
Wetlands' relevance as carbon sinks and climate regulators as dynamic systems of continuous transfer and transformation of GHG, together with their role regulating hydrological flows and buffer extreme events, depends deeply on their health and ecological stability (ref.).

	1.2 Microorganisms & biochemistry
Soil microorganisms drive the principal biogeochemical processes in wetlands, including organic-matter decomposition, nutrient recycling, and regulation of carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), and nitrous oxide (N₂O) fluxes. Bacteria, archaea, and fungi participate in successive transformations of carbon and nitrogen, and their activity is controlled mainly by water-table position, oxygen availability, temperature, nutrient status, pH, vegetation, and the quantity and quality of organic substrates (ref.). Consequently, changes in wetland hydrology alter not only microbial community composition but also the metabolic pathways through which plant-derived carbon and nitrogen are returned to the atmosphere (Gios et al., 2024; Joel and Glina, 2026).
Methane formation begins with the breakdown of dead plant litter, roots, and peat organic matter into simpler compounds. In the initial stage, complex polymers such as cellulose and hemicellulose are hydrolyzed by extracellular enzymes and transformed into soluble compounds. Subsequently, fermentative bacteria convert these monomers into organic acids, alcohols, CO₂, and H₂. 
 Decomposition via primary fermentation
Subsequently, syntrophic and acetogenic microorganisms process these fermentation intermediates, ultimately converting them into acetate, CO₂ and H₂. Once oxygen and other more energetically favorable terminal electron acceptors (such as nitrate, ferric iron, and sulfate) have been completely depleted, methanogenic archaea utilize these end products to generate CH₄.

Methane that escapes oxidation may be emitted by diffusion, ebullition, or transport through the aerenchyma of wetland plants as discussed before. Vegetation can create an efficient pathway for CH₄ transport from peat to the atmosphere by providing methanogenic substrate and introducing oxygen into the rhizosphere (Gutekunst et al., 2022; Gios et al., 2024; Joel and Glina, 2026).
Agricultural practices such as tillage, fertilisation, vegetation removal, and repeated drying can further increase aeration, disrupt peat structure, and expose physically protected organic matter. As decomposition proceeds, peat loses carbon, becomes denser, and subsides; deeper water tables can then maintain a thick oxic layer and reinforce continued peat oxidation. The magnitude of this response nevertheless depends on peat type, temperature, pH, nutrient availability, substrate quality, and vegetation, and cannot be attributed to single aspects as oxygen (Page et al., 2022; Gios et al., 2024; Joel and Glina, 2026).
When drained peat is rewetted, oxygen diffusion declines rapidly and aerobic CO₂ production is generally reduced, but CH₄ emissions may increase markedly, particularly during the first years after restoration. This response can reflect a drainage legacy: aerobic decomposition during the drained phase partially breaks down old peat and plant residues, releases nutrients, and generates soluble or relatively labile carbon compounds. After flooding, this substrate pool becomes available to fermenters and methanogens. At the same time, the expanding anoxic zone favours methanogenesis, while the contraction of the oxygenated surface layer limits aerobic CH₄ oxidation. Flooding may also produce a nutrient-rich layer of decomposing vegetation and detritus that functions as a hotspot of fermentation and CH₄ production (Gios et al., 2024; Joel and Glina, 2026). 
However, high CH₄ emissions are not an inevitable consequence of rewetting. Strongly decomposed peat may contain little readily degradable substrate and methanogenesis may remain limited where alternative electron acceptors are abundant or where methanogenic communities recover slowly (ref.). Post-rewetting emissions therefore vary with peat degradation, water-table stability, vegetation, nutrient status, land-use and drought history, restoration method, and the relative development of methanogenic and methanotrophic communities. Recovery of community composition also does not necessarily imply immediate recovery of ecosystem function or pristine CH₄ fluxes (Gutekunst et al., 2022; Gios et al., 2024). 
1.2.1 Fermentation 
Under persistent anoxic conditions, overall decomposition rates drop precipitously. Highly complex plant polymers (as lignin and crystalline cellulose) cannot be efficiently broken down by the anaerobic enzymatic machinery, resulting in the incomplete degradation of macro-organic matter and driving the progressive accumulation of peat over millennia (Roulet, 2000).
Remaining organic matter ferments via specialized heterotrophs bacteria which unlike aerobic respirers operate in absence of external terminal electron acceptors redistributing electrons within the organic substrate itself to produce finished products (Madigan et al., 2020) (Schlesinger & Bernhardt, 2020).
This fermentation is selective, as it only targets the easily degradable fractions of the organic pool, such as soluble carbohydrates and proteins (Megonigal et al., 2004). Fermenting bacteria quickly transform these simple compounds into short-chain fatty acids, alcohols, H₂, and CO₂. Instead of accumulating, these intermediate products are rapidly consumed as food by downstream anaerobic microorganisms, ultimately fueling methanogenesis (McInerney et al., 2009).

1.2.2 Nitrogen aspects 
Nitrous oxide (N2O) production in peatlands is highly responsive to coupled shifts in soil moisture, oxygen availability, and nutrient status (Tong et al., 2022). The net emission of this potent greenhouse gas is regulated by two primary, sequential microbial pathways operating at distinct redox boundaries:
* Nitrification: Under well-oxygenated conditions, ammonia-oxidizing bacteria and archaea convert ammonium (NH4+) into nitrite (NO2-) and nitrate (NO3-), releasing N2O as a minor metabolic by-product.
* Denitrification: Under oxygen limitation, heterotrophic bacteria and nitrifier-denitrifiers sequentially reduce NO3- to NO2-, nitric oxide (NO), N2O and ultimately molecular nitrogen (N2).

N2O accumulates and escapes into the atmosphere when this enzymatic cascade is interrupted. Such incomplete denitrification occurs under specific environmental stress factors, including highly fluctuating oxic-anoxic cycles, high nitrate supply, unbalanced organic carbon-to-nitrogen ratios, low soil pH, or when the activity of the nitrous oxide reductase enzyme (N2O-reductase) is physiologically suppressed (Gios et al., 2024).
Drained agricultural peatlands can also represent severe hotspots for N2O emissions due to intense land-management practices. Artificial drainage introduces deep oxygen penetration, which accelerates native nitrogen mineralization and nitrification. When these systems are subjected to synthetic fertilization or manure application, they receive an excess loading of NH4+ and NO3-, heavily fueling both production pathways (ref.).


  

	Figure, Agricultural nitrogen inputs in paddy-peatlands | Inputs accelerate the entire nitrogen cycle compared to natural peatlands. In the upper oxic layer, nitrogen input nearly doubles microbial mineralization rates. Extracted from Shi Y., et al. (2021).
	Furthermore, agricultural practices such as straw mulching or crop-residue retention complicate these dynamics. While mulching provides labile organic carbon that can accelerate microbial respiration and draw down oxygen, it can also create highly localized, unbalanced carbon-to-nitrogen microsites that alter denitrification rates and lead to substantial N2O accumulation (ref.).
These management pressures interact dynamically with hydrology. Water-table fluctuations create alternating oxygen-rich (oxic) and oxygen-depleted (anoxic) microsites (Groffman et al., 2009). This micro-zonation tightly couples the system: nitrate generated in oxic pockets is immediately denitrified in adjacent anoxic zones, creating hotspots for outsized N2O emissions. Similar high-emission hotspots occur in wet but unsaturated peat after clear-cutting, where losing tree transpiration triggers water-table instability (Tikkasalo et al., 2025).
Ecological rewetting is generally implemented to mitigate these impacts. Establishing stable, permanent water saturation cuts off oxygen diffusion, completely suppressing nitrification and forcing the consumption and complete reduction of remaining NO3- pools into harmless N2 gas.
Nevertheless, the transition phase of restoration can introduce temporary biogeochemical risks. Severe N2O emission peaks frequently occur during initial reflooding transitions, seasonal water-level drawdowns, or winter freeze–thaw cycles (Liu et al., 2020). Crucially, if a peatland has a long history of heavy fertilization or high nutrient accumulation, the rewetted organic soil can remain a substantial, long-term source of atmospheric N2O as long as residual nitrate loading or background mineralization continues to persist within the flooded matrix (Gios et al., 2024).
1.2.3 Drained peatlands
In Europe, drained peatlands constitute a relatively small proportion of the land area, around a 3% of the total agricultural area (Greifswald Mire Centre, 2023), but they contribute disproportionately to regional GHG emissions (ref.). In Germany, particularly in northern regions such as Schleswig-Holstein, the historical drainage of wetlands for agricultural use has generated highly anthropized landscapes, altering the processes that originally allowed the storage of large quantities of organic carbon (ref.), where hydrological restoration and rewetting have emerged as priority strategies for climate mitigation and ecological recovery (ref.).
As discussed before, the lowering of the water table increases soil aeration which accelerates the aerobic decomposition of previously preserved organic matter, significantly increasing CO2 emissions into the atmosphere. Nevertheless, drainage is a different type of aeration as in general means a complete break with the natural water cycles and usually involves other extensive industrial interventions. These processes turn peatlands into important carbon sources, as the carbon accumulated over millennia begins to mineralize rapidly (ref.) generating that ecosystems that originally acted as carbon sinks can become net emitters of GHG (ref.).
For the same reasons that drainage is carried out (such as establishing an agricultural field), trees and other plants are also often removed. After tree removal, the water table rises (Korkiakoski et al., 2019; Leppä et al., 2020a), which increases CH₄ production in the extended anaerobic space. Then, peatlands transform from net CH4 sinks into sources (Korkiakoski et al., 2019).

These processes utilize terminal electron acceptors to oxidize volatile fermentation products (primarily acetate and H₂) competing directly with downstream methanogenesis for these shared substrates (Megonigal et al., 2004). 1.2.5 Biogeochemical state
Another perspective to observe the biochemistry is the the biogeochemical state, which refers to the dynamic network of interconnected chemical equilibria, nutrient cycles, and microbial metabolic networks that govern elemental transformations in the soil (Megonigal et al., 2004). It is regulated by the sequence of terminal electron accepting processes. Driven by oxygen availability, alternative electron acceptors, and organic carbon dynamics, the soil configures a distinct spatial and temporal gradient ranging from highly oxidizing to strongly reducing environments (Reddy & DeLaune, 2022).
The redox potential Eh serves to quantify this state. It measures the relative activity of electron acceptors and donors, reflecting the predominant microbial pathways operating in the system (Husson, 2013). High Eh values signify oxidizing well-oxygenated conditions where aerobic respiration dominates, mineralising organic matter directly into CO₂.
As water saturation limits oxygen diffusion, facultative and obligate anaerobic microorganisms sequentially utilize alternative electron acceptors based on their free energy yield. This thermodynamic hierarchy known as the "redox ladder" follows a strict sequential depletion of electron acceptors:

Ultimately, the physical positioning of these competing microbial groups across the soil's redox gradient determines the net balance between methane production and consumption before the gas escapes to the atmosphere.

	Fluctuations in electrical conductivity reflect nutrient mobilization, groundwater inflows, and solute dynamics driven by hydrological connectivity (Reddy & DeLaune, 2022). In highly heterogeneous wetland soils, these hydrological shifts create dynamic spatial and temporal gradients in oxygen availability. Sudden flooding, water table drawdowns, or compaction events alter soil porosity, shifting the balance of terminal electron accepting processes and directly controlling the rates of peat degradation (Roulet, 2000). 
Organic acids usually act as the primary, non-gaseous carbon intermediates linking early decomposition to terminal anaerobic pathways (ref.). Produced via microbial fermentation, root exudation or the incomplete degradation of recalcitrant plant matter, these low-molecular-weight compounds serve as critical electron donors for alternative respiration processes, including denitrification, iron reduction, and sulfate reduction (Megonigal et al., 2004). 
The availability and composition of these organic acids directly modulate the soil's background chemical context. As electron acceptors are sequentially consumed can lower local redox boundaries. This triggers transient emission spikes of CH₄, CO₂, and N₂O by rapidly fueling downstream microbial communities.
Among these intermediates, acetate (CH₃COO⁻) serves as one of the most abundant and biogeochemically critical substrate regulating methane dynamics (Conrad, 1999). In the presence of energetically superior electron acceptors, acetate is preferentially intercepted and consumed by iron- or sulfate-reducing bacteria; however, as these alternative acceptors become limiting, it directly fuels acetoclastic methanogenesis. 
In many peatlands and rewetted organic soils, this single substrate accounts for an estimated 60–70% of total methane production (Conrad, 1999, 2005). Ultimately, acetate acts as a master chemical regulator, driving the competitive microbial interactions that determine whether organic carbon is stabilized within the soil matrix or released into the atmosphere as CO₂ or CH₄.
1.3 Rewetting wetlands
Wetland rewetting involves the deliberate restoration of water levels in previously drained or degraded systems, with the aim of recovering their ecological and hydrological functions and their capacity to store carbon. This intervention is part of the passive and active restoration strategies promoted to mitigate the negative effects of drainage, intensive agriculture, and peat extraction, which have turned many wetlands into net sources of CO₂ and other GHG (Kustina et al., 2023; Darusman et al., 2023).
  

	Figure, Restoration and water draining | how restoring peatlands improves their ability to manage water during rain events. Rewetting helps restore the peat structure, making it less dense with higher water storage capacity, which slows down the groundwater table's response to rain and provides an improved hydrological buffer function (Ahmad S., et al., 2020). 
	1.3.1 Rewetting, methodological aspects
Wetting drained wetlands essentially involves raising and stabilizing the water table to near-natural levels, reducing soil oxygenation, and restoring biogeochemical processes characteristic of functional wetlands (ref.).
The most common rewetting technique consists of blocking or dismantling artificial drainage systems (ditches, canals, or underground pipes) to decrease water runoff (ref.). This is achieved through the construction of dikes, earth dams, wooden structures, or synthetic barriers.
In agricultural contexts or exploited peatlands, rewetting can also involve reshaping the microrelief and restoring lateral hydrological connectivity, facilitating seasonal flooding and surface water retention (ref.). Other examples can be more harsh, as those on which they burn the present plants during rewetting to apparently control the emissions (Cui S., et al., 2025).
In some cases, particularly in coastal wetlands or those of northern Germany, the controlled introduction of brackish or fresh water has been used as a rewetting strategy, which introduces additional considerations related to the sulfur cycle and salinity (Pönisch et al., 2025).
  

	Figure, Rewetting scenarios | A polemic comparison between greenhouse gas emissions across No rewetting and direct rewetting scenarios (Cui S., et al., 2025).
	1.3.2 Rewetting effects
As has been observed, restoring soil moisture reduces CO₂ emissions, primarily by limiting oxygen uptake into the soil and, consequently, the aerobic respiration of organic matter, which is the main source of CO₂ in drained soils (ref.). 
A recent meta-analysis showed that rewetting degraded peatlands can decrease CO₂ emission rates by approximately −1.43 ± 0.36 g CO₂ (per meter square day), with particularly significant effects in temperate zones where restored hydrological conditions prevent the aerobic mineralization of organic carbon (Darusman et al., 2023). 
However, the most concerning aspect of the rewetting process is that it often increases methane emissions due to the establishment of anoxic conditions that favor the activity of methanogenic archaea. The same meta-analysis found that CH₄ emission rates in rewetted wetlands were significantly higher than in drained areas, especially in the first few years after rewetting (Kalhori et al., 2024) with an effect that remained consistent across different climatic zones (Darusman et al., 2023).

	1.3.3 Underlying ecological mechanisms
Rewetting wetlands can take decades to stabilize, which reflects on GHG fluctuations cycles before approaching new equilibriums, underscoring the importance of long-term monitoring to assess the success of restoration (Evans et al., 2021).
For developing an effective rewetting, a methodological evaluation design which includes not only hydrological aspects but also biogeochemical and ecological ones is required. The conditions in which plant species develop and enter into phases of ecological succession (or not) are key in the regulation of the biogeochemical conditions on which occurs [q]the gas exchange. Comprehending the ecosystem ecology and the close relationships between the living organisms is essential to be able to comprehend the scenario that will generate emission or absorption or exchange of gases.
The mechanisms explaining these changes in emissions are related to soil redox conditions and the structure of the microbial community after groundwater restoration. The reintroduction of water favors anoxic environments, where anaerobic processes such as methanogenesis predominate, compared to the aerobic processes that dominated in drained soils, accelerating CH₄ production. Oxygen limitation decreases the activity of aerobic microorganisms responsible for mineralization and CO₂ release (Kustina et al., 2025). 
Post-rewetting hydrological variability generates a mosaic of microenvironments with different redox states, which can favor the coexistence of anaerobic methane oxidation (ANME) and aerobic methanotrophy in areas where oxygen diffuses temporarily, modulating net CH₄ emissions (Turner et al., 2023; Kustina et al., 2025).
The response of rewetted wetlands is not uniform over time or space. Long-term studies have shown that gas emissions can evolve for decades after restoration, gradually approaching functional patterns similar to those of undisturbed wetlands, especially in terms of CO₂ and CH₄ balances (ref.).[r]
For example, in rewetted peatlands in Germany, a transition of net carbon emissions towards more neutral balances has been observed over periods of 13–16 years after restoration, although with interannual variability associated with extreme weather events or hydrological fluctuations (Kalhori et al., 2024).
Furthermore, the system's response depends on the regional climate context, land-use history, and vegetation and microbiota characteristics present before restoration (ref.). The heterogeneity of these factors means that the effects of rewetting vary between sites, underscoring the need for adaptive strategies based on continuous monitoring and robust quantitative approaches.

1.4 Physical and micrometeorological controls of gas exchange and variables[v]
The magnitude, direction, and temporal variability of the observed fluxes are strongly modulated by physical state of the system (ref.) and the micrometeorological conditions: small-scale weather processes and atmospheric phenomena occurring in the layer of air nearest to the Earth’s surface, basically between surface and the atmosphere, intensely shaped by the surrounding physical environment.[w] Between its components are turbulent fluxes, microclimates, soil conditions & atmospheric stability (ref). So on, it is essential to understand the physical and micrometeorological variables that regulate wetland dynamics (ref.).
Physical variables, and particularly micrometeorological approaches such as EC, require an understanding of how the internal physics of the wetland ecosystem (ref.) develops and is controlled in the atmosphere, soil, and transition zones (ref.), as these govern both gas production and consumption, as well as their transport. The most relevant variables are listed below:

	1.4.3 Evapotranspiration 
ET is a fundamental component of the terrestrial water and energy cycles, representing the total flux of water transferred from the land surface to the atmosphere. It comprises two distinct processes: physical evaporation from soil or surface water, and biological transpiration through plant stomata. In agricultural and wetland-transition ecosystems, ET is a critical driver of the surface energy balance, directly consuming available energy in the form of LE.
ET links the hydrological cycle with the carbon cycle; the same stomatal pathways that release transpired water also regulate the uptake of carbon dioxide for photosynthesis (GPP). 
The ecosystem's response to atmospheric water demand—measured as Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD)—dictates stomatal conductance, making ET a key variable for assessing drought stress, eWUE, and the overall resilience of the crop to micrometeorological extremes.

	1.4.4 Turbulence and fluxes 
The atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) is a transition zone, which shows a daily temperature cycle (unlike the upper "free troposphere") and the lowest part of the troposphere; ranges between 1 to 2 km; is turbulent and in direct contact with the Earth's surface, facilitating the transport of heat, moisture and pollutants, also reacting to surface forcings such as heat and friction on a time basis (ref.).
During the day, surface heating creates a thicker mixed layer, while at night, cooling forms a stable layer, leaving a residual layer above (ref.). Gases are transformed in the ABL according to the dynamics between wetlands and the atmosphere (ref.).
Integrating continuous ABL measurements with tower-based EC flux measurements represents a fundamental advance in understanding land-atmosphere interactions within the context of GHG monitoring (Helbig et al., 2021). 
  
[aa]
	Figure, Planet Boundary Layer | A diurnal (24-hour) cycle and structure of the PBL, showing how solar heating and radiative cooling dynamically change the lower atmosphere.
	The ABL directly influenced by the surface, mediates the exchange of energy, matter (CO₂, water, CH₄) and momentum between ecosystems and the free troposphere, thereby controlling key processes such as cloud formation, pollutant dispersion, and carbon storage (Helbig et al., 2021). 
Its diurnal evolution shifting between the daytime convective boundary layer (CBL) and the nighttime stable boundary layer (NBL), determines the vertical structure of fluxes and GHG storage within the boundary layer itself (Helbig et al., 2021).
Turbulence is the dynamic process that governs transport, characterized by chaotic fluctuations in wind speed that allow for the vertical mixing of heat, water vapor, and gases. Thus, turbulence acts as the link between the biogeochemical processes occurring in the soil and the signals observed in the atmosphere (ref.).
Mechanical turbulence in the low atmosphere is produced primarily by wind shear and surface friction as airflow interacts with rough terrain, vegetation, buildings, and other obstacles. This shear-driven turbulence generates turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) through the mechanical production term in the TKE budget and is especially important near the surface and under stable or neutral stratification (Honnert et al., 2020). 
  

	Figure, Turbulence in low atmosphere | Thermal (C) and mechanical (A and B) turbulence in lower atmospheric layers. Extracted from meteoswiss. 
	In contrast, thermal turbulence is generated by buoyancy when the surface is heated, creating rising warm air parcels and sinking cooler air; this convective mixing dominates during daytime over land when the ABL is unstable (Honnert et al., 2020; Karasewicz et al., 2024).
During the day, buoyancy-driven (thermal) turbulence is typically the main source of mixing, while mechanical (shear) turbulence supplements it and can tilt or weaken thermals; both processes operate together to determine the ABL structure (Honnert et al., 2020). 
At night, surface cooling creates stable stratification that suppresses buoyancy production, so turbulence is then mostly shear-driven and confined closer to the ground (Karasewicz et al., 2024). The relative importance of thermal versus mechanical production is often expressed by the Richardson number or the Monin–Obukhov length, which quantify how stratification and shear jointly control turbulence intensity and vertical mixing in the low atmosphere (Honnert et al., 2020).
Conditions of high H are associated with more unstable atmospheres and, therefore, with greater turbulent mixing (ref.). In wetlands, where water availability is high, LE flux often represents a significant fraction of the energy balance, contributing to regulating air humidity and cooling the surface (ref.).
The friction velocity (u*) measures the intensity of turbulence and is a micrometeorological parameter that represents the efficiency of turbulent transport near the surface (ref.). High u* values ​​indicate vigorous atmospheric mixing, which favors gas exchange between the ecosystem and the atmosphere (ref.).
In contrast, low u* values, common at night or during periods of atmospheric stability, limit vertical mixing, causing gas accumulation near the ground and resulting in underestimated flow measurements (ref.). For this reason, u* is a fundamental criterion for quality control of EC data.
Wind speed and direction also play a role in flow dynamics. Wind speed modulates the generation of mechanical turbulence, especially under conditions of low thermal convection, while wind direction determines the source area or footprint of the measurements (ref.).
In heterogeneous ecosystems such as wetlands, where microforms coexist (e.g., elevated areas and flooded depressions), the wind direction determines which portion of the landscape contributes to the observed flow, affecting its spatial interpretation (ref.).
1.4.5 Atmospheric conditions 
Naturally, atmospheric conditions near the surface play a fundamental role in gas exchange processes in wetlands. In general, an increase in temperature increases biological activity, accelerating both carbon fixation and release (ref.). Air temperature (and its potential increase) can alter the metabolic rates of plants and microorganisms depending on the availability of water, nutrients, and soil redox conditions (e.g., photosynthesis, respiration, and decomposition of organic matter) (ref.). It also affects anaerobic processes such as methanogenesis due to the close relationship between air and soil temperature (ref.).
Relative humidity (RH) describes the water vapor content in the air relative to its maximum capacity at a given temperature. Its ecological interpretation is effective when integrated with air temperature in the VPD (ref.).
VPD is one of the most relevant variables for understanding the interaction between the atmosphere and vegetation, represents the difference between the amount of water vapor present in the air and the amount it could contain if it were saturated, a direct indicator of the evaporative demand of the atmosphere (ref.). 
High VPD values ​​indicate dry conditions that increase water loss through transpiration, which can induce stomatal closure in plants (ref.). This closure reduces CO₂ uptake for photosynthesis, decreasing gross primary production and altering the net carbon balance of the ecosystem (ref.).

	At the atmospheric level, wind contributes to the ventilation of the system, with its speed serving as an indicator (ref.). Wind also affects moisture distribution, surface evaporation, and coupling between the surface and the atmosphere (ref.).

In practice, EC measurements almost universally suffer from the energy balance closure (EBC) problem, where the measured turbulent fluxes (LE+H) account for 10% to 30% less energy than the available thermodynamic energy (Rn−G). 
In complex, transitional, or rewetted ecosystems (such as the Klimafarm cropland), this lack of closure is primarily driven by three micrometeorological phenomena
1. Large-scale Eddies (Secondary Circulations): Uneven heating of heterogeneous terrain generates massive, slow-moving thermal convection cells. Standard EC software (like EddyPro®) calculates fluxes based on 30-minute block averages. These massive eddies often take longer than 30 minutes to pass, causing a severe underestimation of the low-frequency turbulent transport of H and LE (ref.).[ac][ad]
2. Hidden Heat Storage: Standard models measure G using soil heat plates. However, in rewetted or periodically flooded croplands, standing water and saturated soil profiles act as massive thermal sinks. A significant portion of Rn is diverted into heating this water column or crop biomass—terms that are frequently unmeasured and erroneously assigned to the missing energy fraction(ref.).
3. Advection and the Oasis Effect: EC assumes a flat, infinite, and homogeneous fetch (a 1D vertical model). In heterogeneous landscapes, where wet patches neighbor dry fields, H is transported horizontally by wind (advection). This hot, dry air forces evaporation in the wetland (LE), but the horizontal movement of the H passes unmeasured by the vertical anemometer, breaking the local energy balance equation.[ae]

	Advection, defined as the horizontal transport of heat and moisture by moving air masses or shallow surface water, can introduce or remove energy from the measurement footprint without being detected by conventional EC instrumentation, which primarily quantifies vertical turbulent fluxes (Foken, 2008). 
Intermittent standing water and saturated peat layers can store substantial amounts of thermal energy during daytime heating and release this energy during cooling periods. If these storage terms are not explicitly quantified, a portion of the available energy remains unaccounted for, contributing to the observed EBC gap (Wilson et al., 2002).
Furthermore, drained peatlands behave similarly to warmer surfaces, where dry peat rapidly heats during daytime and enhances sensible heat fluxes and turbulent mixing. In contrast, rewetted peatlands resemble cooler surfaces, as saturated peat, mosses, and open water promote evaporative cooling and reduce daytime surface temperatures. 
When relatively warm air from surrounding drained or agricultural areas advects over the cooler rewetted surface, sensible heat fluxes and thermal turbulence are suppressed, weakening vertical mixing and the entrainment of drier air from above. Combined with the increased atmospheric humidity generated by enhanced ET, this may favor the persistence of low-level clouds, mist, or fog over restored peatlands, contributing to the formation of a localized “cool and humid island” microclimate.[af]
1.4.7 Gas Exchange & Ecohydrological Coupling[ag]
To fully interpret the micrometeorological fluxes measured by the tower, it is crucial to understand the sequential coupling between soil physical constraints and the biological regulation of gas exchange. The linkage between hydrology, thermodynamics, and ecosystem physiology is governed by several critical drivers:
VPD[ah] represents the atmospheric "thirst" for water, driven by high air temperatures and low relative humidity. It is one of the most relevant variables for understanding the interaction between atmosphere and vegetation. 
High VPD values ​​indicate dry conditions that increase water loss through transpiration, which can induce stomatal closure in plants (ref.). Extreme VPD induces severe atmospheric drought while a moderate VPD encourages steady photosynthetic carbon uptake (GPP), 
The closure of stomates from high VPD reduces CO₂ uptake for photosynthesis, decreasing gross primary production and altering the net carbon balance of the ecosystem (ref.). It is the primary atmospheric driver of transpiration. To prevent catastrophic hydraulic failure, plants forcefully close [ai]their stomata, abruptly curtailing transpiration. Because CO2 and H2O share the same stomatal pathway, this defensive closure simultaneously halts carbon assimilation, causing GPP to collapse.
The Bowen Ratio (β): Defined as the ratio of H to LE, indicates how the ecosystem partitions its available surface energy. When the soil is well-aerated (uncompacted) and plants have access to water, LE (evaporation and transpiration) dominates, resulting in β<1.

* However, if severe soil compaction restricts root water access, or if extreme VPD forces stomatal closure, the ecosystem can no longer dissipate solar radiation via transpiration. This excess energy is aggressively redirected into Sensible Heat (H), violently warming the surrounding air. 
Consequently, a Bowen ratio spiking above 1.0 serves as a direct thermodynamic alarm indicating severe physiological shutdown.

	Ecosystem Water Use Efficiency (eWUE): eWUE is the ratio of carbon assimilated to water lost (GPP/ET). It serves as the ultimate cost-benefit metric of the ecosystem. Under optimal conditions, the canopy maximizes carbon uptake for every unit of transpired water. However, when the soil macro-porosity is compromised (e.g., exceeding precompression limits), the physical restriction on water and gas movement forces the ecosystem into a highly inefficient state, drastically reducing eWUE.
By integrating these metrics, the EC system effectively maps the unseen mechanical and hydrological limitations of the soil profile into observable, quantified fluxes of energy and GHG.
1.5 Soil Physical and Hydrological Controls[ak]
While the role of wetland soil as an ecosystem element has already been discussed in the chapter “The Wetland Continuum”, it is necessary to go deeper into the physical variables that regulate water movement, gas diffusion, oxygen availability, and the development of biogeochemical processes.
Soil physical phenomena not only affects the mechanical stability but also the ecological functioning of the wetland and carbon and methane dynamics (ref.). The hydrological and mechanical properties of the soil are closely coupled (ref.). 
Water content modifies the soil's structural strength, while changes in compaction alter porosity, hydraulic conductivity, and water storage capacity (ref.). Phenomena such as subsidence, pore collapse, or compaction can transform oxygenation dynamics and the spatial distribution of aerobic and anaerobic environments, profoundly altering CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O emissions (ref.).
1.5.1 Soil–hydrological controls 
The water table depth (WTD) is probably the most influential variable in gas dynamics in wetlands. It defines the position of the air-water interface within the soil profile and, therefore, the thickness of the oxygenated zone (ref.).
When the water table is near or above the surface, oxygen diffusion is severely restricted, favoring reducing conditions that promote anaerobic processes such as fermentation, the reduction of inorganic compounds, and methanogenesis (ref.). In contrast, when the water table drops, oxygen penetration into the soil increases, stimulating aerobic processes such as microbial respiration and methane oxidation (ref.). Thus, small variations in WTD can generate significant changes in the magnitude and direction of CH₄ and CO₂ fluxes.
  

	  

	Figures, Soil Water Content (SWC) | (Left) SWC influencing microbial communities and ecosystem health in wetlands. (Right) SWC and availability of water for plants related to the Water Table. Extracted from Luo, S. (2025) & ConnectedCrops.ca. 
	Soil Water Content (SWC) describes the proportion of water retained in soil pores, directly influencing gas diffusion. High SWC values ​​reduce the connectivity of air-filled pores, limiting oxygen transport and favoring anaerobic conditions even when the water table is not at the surface (ref.). Conversely, lower SWC values ​​allow for greater aeration, facilitating aerobic processes and methane oxidation (ref.).
Soil temperature (ST) acts as a direct regulator of microbial activity and biochemical reaction rates (ref.). In general, increased ST accelerates metabolic processes, including the decomposition of organic matter and methane production (ref.). 
Water temperature (WT), meanwhile, influences gas solubility, the kinetics of microbial reactions, and diffusion processes between water and the atmosphere (ref.). In addition, the water temperature can differ from that of the air and the soil, generating thermal microenvironments that affect biological activity in a differentiated way (ref.).
1.5.2 Soil pore functionality and gas transport
The porous structure of the soil is one of the main factors controlling the physical functioning of wetlands. The size, distribution, and connectivity of the pores determine the soil's capacity to store and transport water, air, and solutes, simultaneously regulating oxygen diffusion, nutrient mobility, and gas exchange with the atmosphere (ref.). In saturated systems such as wetlands, where small variations in oxygen availability can profoundly modify biogeochemical processes, the functionality of the pore system plays a central role in carbon and methane dynamics (ref.).
The Bulk Density, is one of the most relevant physical properties. Corresponds to the ratio between the dry mass of the soil and its total volume, including the pore space. Natural organic soils typically have low bulk densities due to their high organic matter content and high porosity (ref.). However, processes such as drainage, peat oxidation, and compaction progressively increase bulk density, reducing pore volume and altering the soil's capacity to retain water and allow gas exchange (ref.).
Soil pores can be broadly classified as macropores and micropores, according to their size and function (ref.). Macropores are relatively large spaces, frequently associated with roots, biological channels, and structural aggregates, which facilitate the rapid movement of water and air (ref.). These pores are essential for soil aeration and the efficient transport of oxygen to surface layers. Conversely, micropores store water retained by capillary forces and exhibit much slower circulation, favoring the persistence of saturated conditions and limiting gas diffusion (ref.).
  
  
  

	Figure, Structure of soil porosity | (Left) Highlighting the distinction between macropores and micropores based on size and function & (Right) Complex micro-architecture of soil, highlighting how physical, chemical, and biological components interact to create a functional ecosystem. Extracted from Latshaw (2009); Fonte, S. J., (2009).
	Pore ​​connectivity is equally important, as it determines the continuity of transport pathways within the soil (ref.). A highly connected pore system facilitates the circulation of water and gases, while the loss of connectivity resulting from compaction, structural collapse, or permanent saturation, restricts oxygen movement and favors the formation of anaerobic microenvironments (ref.). In wetlands, the coexistence of connected and disconnected zones generates high spatial heterogeneity, allowing aerobic and anaerobic processes to occur simultaneously at millimeter scales (ref.).
Atmospheric oxygen moves primarily through air-filled pores, so water saturation drastically reduces its diffusion rate (ref.). Diffusion in water is several orders of magnitude slower. As a result, even small amounts of water occupying the pores can severely limit oxygen uptake by the soil, favoring anaerobic processes such as fermentation and methanogenesis (ref.). Similarly, the porous structure controls the capacity of CH₄ produced at depth to reach the surface and be emitted into the atmosphere (ref.).
Water transport within the soil is governed by hydraulic gradients and the geometry of the pore system (ref.). The soil's capacity to transmit water is expressed by hydraulic conductivity, a property that describes the ease with which water can move through the soil profile (ref.). Hydraulic conductivity in wetlands is often highly variable due to structural heterogeneity and the presence of layers with different degrees of decomposition (ref.).
The functionality of the pore system not only determines the movement of water and air, but also the spatial distribution of oxidizing and reducing environments, affecting the balance between greenhouse gas production and consumption (ref.).
1.5.3 Soil mechanical behavior and compaction
Wetland soils, especially those rich in organic matter and peat, possess significantly different mechanical properties than many mineral soils (ref.). Their high porosity, high water content, and abundance of partially decomposed organic material result in a highly compressible structure susceptible to deformation in response to changes in load or drainage (ref.).
The soil exhibits mechanical behavior that responds to external forces such as the weight and movement of agricultural machinery, the passage of animals, sediment accumulation, and variations in water content (ref.). An important part of all these processes involves the properties of wetland pores and the pressure they contain (ref.). When a soil is subjected to a load, part of the pressure is borne by the water contained in the pores and another part by the solid soil matrix (ref.).


  

	Figure, Effect of mechanical loading on physical soil properties | Modified after Rode J. H. (2024).
	This is known as effective effort, the real supported load by the mineral structure of the soil, excluding the water pressure on pores (ref.). In that sense, the water can partially reduce the stress of the solid matrix (ref.). This is what governs the porous structure deformation, the strength of the material and particle and aggregates reorganization (ref.).
The application of mechanical loads to the soil causes compaction—that is, a reduction in the soil's total volume (ref.). This process entails a decrease in porosity and an increase in bulk density, particularly affecting the macropores responsible for air and water circulation (ref.). In functional terms, compaction limits infiltration, reduces hydraulic conductivity, and hinders oxygen diffusion to deeper layers (ref.). Consequently, compaction directly alters soil redox conditions and modifies microbial activity associated with the production and consumption of GHG (ref.).

	In peatlands and organic soils, compaction can be accompanied by subsidence, that is, a progressive lowering of the ground surface (ref.). Subsidence results from the combination of:
i) physical loss of volume,
ii) oxidative decomposition of organic matter,
iii) structural consolidation (ref.).
The mechanical response of the soil also depends strongly on its water content (ref.). Wetter soils tend to have lower mechanical strength and greater susceptibility to deformation, while drier soils can develop more rigid structures but are also more fragile and prone to collapse (ref.). This interaction between moisture and mechanical strength represents a feedback loop between hydrology and physical structure, which is stronger in systems subjected to drainage and rewetting cycles (ref.).

The reduction of macropores limits oxygen transport and favors persistent anaerobic environments, while alterations in the connectivity of the porous system affect the diffusion and emission of CH₄ and CO₂ (ref.).

These curves allow for the quantification of soil susceptibility to structural deformations and are a key indicator of the physical stability of wetlands subjected to drainage, agricultural use, or hydrological restoration (ref.). Generally speaking, a compression curve represents the relationship between applied stress and soil deformation (ref.), usually expressed as changes in bulk density, specific volume, or void ratio (ref.).

However, when loads exceed this threshold, a permanent reorganization of the porous structure occurs, initiating irreversible compaction processes (ref.).

	Compression curves also allow the identification of stages in the mechanical behavior of the soil, with their respective biogeochemical implications: Initially, under low loads, small deformations predominate, related to particle readjustment and the expulsion of air from large pores (ref.). Subsequently, upon overcoming the pre-compression stress, a virgin compression phase begins, characterized by a more pronounced reduction in volume due to the structural collapse of pores and aggregates (ref.), which can imply significant losses of hydrological functionality and aeration capacity (ref.).

	The transition of this process can be described and evaluated—specifically regarding soil stability—when an external load acts upon organic soil, such as peat. The core concept is that if the applied stress is below the preconsolidation pressure (PC), the soil behaves elastically, and no significant changes are expected. If the load exceeds that preconsolidation pressure, the soil undergoes plastic deformation, resulting in changes to porosity, air capacity, and conductivity.
Based on this, there are four scenarios:
1. σ1 < PC: The soil remains within its stability range. There are no significant changes, and trafficability is maintained.
2. σ1 > PC: The load exceeds the preconsolidation stress (PC). Hydraulic conductivity (Ks) and air capacity (AC) decrease, while bulk density (ρB) and effective preconsolidation stress increase.
3. Heavy shearing, failure of bearing capacity: Here, it is no longer just a matter of compaction, there is bearing capacity failure and intense shearing. This results in a severe loss of soil functions and marked rutting or subsidence.
4. High water content or homogenization: With high water content, the soil becomes more homogeneous and loses structural differentiation. Ks, AC, ρB, and PC decrease, but the mechanism differs because excess water destroys structural stability.
The consequences for soil functions involve changes in porosity, aeration, and conductivity. If the load remains within limits, the soil is preserved; if the preconsolidation pressure is exceeded, subsidence or a loss of load-bearing capacity occurs. In other words, the soil can withstand a load without serious damage only as long as the applied pressure does not exceed its pre-existing strength. When that limit is surpassed, the pore system collapses, and the soil deforms and loses functionality.

	1.5.5 Effect of Drainage
In wetlands and peatlands, pre-compression stress is usually relatively low due to high porosity and high organic matter content, making these soils particularly vulnerable to mechanical disturbances associated with drainage, heavy machinery, or agricultural activities (ref.).
Drained peatlands experience a disconnection from the natural water regime, causing a drop in the water table and decreasing hydraulic support, thus increasing the effective pressure on the soil structure. As a consequence, drained organic soils suffer less water regulation capacity and greater thermal fluctuations (ref.) reducing the resilience of the ecosystem to droughts and extreme climatic events (ref.); macropores collapse (ref.) and soils become more vulnerable to processes of structural soil degradation, compaction, consolidation (ref.) potentially decreasing their elevation by several centimeters per year (ref.).
With the partial loss of soil structure, runoff increases (ref.). Due to runoff, dissolved organic carbon (DOC) is lost through drainage ditches, further depleting the soil's carbon stock (ref.). Consolidation theory states that as time passes, water drains outwards and this excess pressure is reduced to zero or almost zero, at which point the load is supported primarily by the solid skeleton of the soil (ref.).
The “consolidation time” is the period it takes for that excess pore pressure to dissipate (ref.). It does not occur instantaneously, because it depends on: the permeability of the soil, the distance the water must travel to drain, and the drainage conditions of the ground (ref.). When water leaves the pores, the volume of voids decreases, the soil settles, the effective stress between the particles increases and the soil's resistance increases (ref.).


1.6 AIMS 
Drainage, intensive agriculture, and rewetting should therefore be understood as ecological filters that restructure microbial communities and their functions. Drainage commonly decreases obligately anaerobic groups such as methanogens while favouring aerobic bacteria and fungi; effects on overall diversity differ between bogs and fens and among management histories. 
Rewetting promotes succession toward anaerobic and wetland-adapted communities, but complete recovery can take years and may remain constrained by irreversible changes in peat chemistry, structure, nutrient availability, and vegetation. Restoring a stable high water table and appropriate peat-forming vegetation is consequently essential for reducing long-term CO₂ and N₂O losses, rebuilding carbon storage, and managing the potential increase in CH₄ emissions (Gios et al., 2024; Joel and Glina, 2026).
