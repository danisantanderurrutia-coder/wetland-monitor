Abstract
Wetlands are critical ecosystems for global carbon dynamics and so, for Climate Change, functioning simultaneously as carbon sinks or sources. Restoration initiatives, such as rewetting them, seek to recover ecological functions of drained peatlands; however, such interventions can generate greenhouse gas net emissions. This study quantifies Carbon Dioxide (CO₂), Nitrous Oxide (N2O) and Methane (CH₄) exchanges wetlands under restoration from Schleswig-Holstein (S-H) North Germany, using EC (EC) and in situ gas chambers, integrating continuous ecosystem-level flux measurements with fine-scale local observations. 
A mixed and quantitative approach is employed to analyze variability of Greenhouse Gases (GHG) emissions and to understand environmental drivers of flux patterns. Evidence suggests that rewetting increases methane emissions while modulating CO₂ uptake, highlighting the need for integrative monitoring strategies to balance climate mitigation goals with wetland restoration. The findings contribute to filling critical knowledge gaps regarding ecosystem-scale GHG dynamics in temperate rewetting projects and provide methodological guidance for combining EC and other methodologies as chamber-based measurements in North European peatlands.
Keywords: wetlands, greenhouse gas fluxes, methane, carbon dioxide, EC, rewetting, northern Germany

I N T R O D U C T I O N 
Wetlands represent some of the most productive and biogeochemically dynamic ecosystems on Earth. Their unique hydrological regimes create conditions that both store carbon in soils and release potent greenhouse gases such as CH₄[a] and CO₂ (Mitsch & Gosselink, 2015). The balance between sources and sinks, determines whether a wetland acts as a net carbon sink or source, making them critical in climate change mitigation strategies. 
Globally, peatland soils store more than 6.5 x 1010 t. of carbon, more than half the Carbon from the atmosphere (FAO, 2020). In Europe, the estimated peatland Carbon stock is 4.3 x 1010 t., with a total peatland area of around 58.8 Mha, 46% of which is drained (UNEP, 2022)
Recent restoration initiatives through rewetting aim to restore natural hydrological conditions, enhance biodiversity, and re-establish carbon storage functions. However, rewetting can lead to rapid changes in GHG fluxes: while CO₂ uptake may increase due to revived primary production (ref.), anaerobic conditions favor methanogenesis, potentially raising CH₄ emissions (Pönisch et al., 2025). 
Despite extensive research in boreal and tropical wetlands, there still exists an important gap regarding the understanding of temperate rewetting projects, quantifying the dynamics of CO₂ and CH₄ at multiple spatial and temporal scales under different context and scenarios (ref.).
In Europe, and particularly in Germany, peatland rewetting is framed within climate mitigation and biodiversity conservation strategies, such as the Peatland Strategy and the commitments stemming from the Paris Agreement. 
In temperate regions like Schleswig-Holstein, northern Germany, many wetlands have been drained for agriculture or peat extraction, resulting in carbon losses and altered ecosystem functioning (Holl, Pfeiffer, & Kutzbach, 2020).
Particularly relevant meteorological aspects are involved at the North German peatlands, as they are at the temperate climate zone with moderate seasonality: 600–800 mm annual precipitation, and limited temperature extremes. Phenomena such as Nocturn radiative cooling (ref.), fog formation (ref.) and drainage flows (ref.) creates strongly evolving boundary layer structures (ref.). [b]
Rewetting methodologies are being implemented, overcoming challenges of highly altered landscapes, existing infrastructure, different land uses and socioeconomic constraints. Residual ditches, uneven peat subsidence or patchy vegetation are critical aspects of environmental research in this context as they shape, on a higher degree, the hydrological connectivity and emission spatial distribution (ref.). [c]
Part of the initiatives to face those challenges is Klimafarm, a pioneering pilot project in Schleswig-Holstein, Germany, that aims to reconcile climate protection with economically sustainable agriculture on re-wetted peatlands. By restoring previously drained peatlands, which are significant sources of greenhouse gas emissions, the project transforms these landscapes into carbon sinks and valuable habitats while maintaining their agricultural use. 
Central to the initiative is paludiculture, the cultivation of wetland-adapted plants such as reeds, sedges, and rushes. In partnership with Christian-Albrechts-Universität zu Kiel, researchers monitor greenhouse gas emissions, hydrology, and biodiversity to evaluate the environmental benefits of peatland restoration.

	Traditional measurements of GHG fluxes include in situ gas chambers, which in some occasions can capture other aspects of the fine-scale variability on gasses, and EC systems, which provide continuous ecosystem-integrated flux estimations (Baldocchi, 2014). These methodologies allow the integration of signals at different spatiotemporal scales, capturing continuous flows and focal processes, especially relevant for evaluating the balance between CO₂ reduction and CH₄ increase after restoration (Evans et al., 2021).
A useful perspective on this matter research-regarded is to understand how the carbon balance develops in transitioning agricultural landscapes; translating soil physics into ecosystem-level carbon budgets being aware of its challenges.
A mixed and quantitative approach that combines EC and chamber-based methods is applied to close these gaps, by investigating the GHG flux dynamics in rewetting projects of northern German wetlands. 
Searches to quantify temporal patterns of CO₂ and CH₄ fluxes, evaluate the effects of rewetting on net ecosystem carbon balance and identify key environmental drivers influencing gas exchanges. Or in other words, the methodological advancement of GHG monitoring and enhanced understanding of ecosystem responses in transitional peatlands from particular temperate regions.[e]
To address this, this thesis is structured around three central hypotheses derived from site-field dynamics and methodological constraints emerged from the micrometeorological workflow:[f]
1. Comprehensive GHG Shift & Rewetting Trajectory 
Evaluating only the CO2 balance could overestimate the short-term climate mitigation potential of the site(s) on Klimafarm. The integration of non-CO2 GHG (CH4 and N2O) together with the literature analysis reveal the ecosystem as a transient net climate warming source due to the initial methane spike typical of recent peatland rewetting. 
However, it is believed by the author that this is a transitional state; as the ecosystem evolves over the long term (e.g., >10 years), methane emissions are expected to stabilize, eventually allowing the system to transition into a net carbon sink.
2. Algorithmic Uncertainty 
Conventional algorithms for filling in missing data, such as Marginal Distribution Sampling (MDS) perform very well under normal conditions. However, during prolonged sensor outages or in winter when the plant is inactive, these algorithms can generate erroneous, artificial data. Therefore, to reliably calculate the annual carbon balance, it is essential to verify these results using approaches with other models.
3. Exploratory Ecohydrological Relationships 
Given the physical complexity of the transitioning cropland and the lack of vegetational data, we take an inductive, exploratory approach to the ecosystem's physiological responses. We aim to explore and identify potential relationships between the net CO2 sequestration capacity (NEE), energy partitioning (Bowen ratio), and their dependence on dynamic environmental drivers—specifically exploring how localized groundwater fluctuations and atmospheric water demand (VPD) that might relate to stomatal closure and carbon uptake limitations.
To test these hypotheses,[g] the primary objectives of the study are:
1. To[h] integrate footprint-level EC fluxes with localized static chamber measurements to determine the comprehensive Net Greenhouse Gas Balance (NGB) and its equivalent radiative forcing over 20-year and 100-year horizons, contextualizing these within the expected timeline of peatland rewetting.
2. To quantify the continuous annual CO2 budget using EC, isolating true physiological responses from gap-filling artifacts through the deployment of multi-model cross-validation and regional weather proxy data, evaluating the error and the flagging process. 
3. To conduct an exploratory analysis correlating localized groundwater fluctuations and atmospheric drought with the partitioning of available energy as evapotranspiration (ET) vs. sensible heat, Ecosystem Water Use Efficiency (eWUE), and Gross Primary Productivity (GPP).
